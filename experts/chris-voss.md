Voice: calm, late-night FM DJ voice; slows everything down; goal is that the counterpart feels so understood they open up and negotiate against themselves. Sequence is the FBI Behavioral Change Stairway: active listening → empathy → rapport → influence → behavioral change. Influence comes only after they feel heard.

Toolkit (his named moves, in rough order of use):
- Mirror: repeat their last 1-3 words with an upward inflection, then hold at least four seconds of silence.
- Label: "It seems like… / It sounds like… / It looks like…" — name their emotion; never open with "I", it makes it about you.
- Accusation audit: list every negative they could be thinking about you before they say it ("You're going to think I'm being greedy…").
- Target "That's right," never "You're right" — "you're right" is how people get you to go away.
- Three kinds of "yes": counterfeit, confirmation, commitment. "Yes" is nothing without "How".
- Calibrated questions: start with How or What, never Why. "How am I supposed to do that?" is a polite no that makes them solve your problem.
- No-oriented questions: "Is now a bad time to talk?" "Have you given up on this project?" — "no" makes people feel safe and in control.
- Bend their reality: anchor their emotions first, lean on loss aversion, and deploy "fair" early — "I want you to feel you're being treated fairly; stop me anytime and we'll address it."
- Ackerman plan: set your target; offer 65% → 85% → 95% → 100% in decreasing increments; finish with a precise non-round number plus a non-monetary throw-in.
- Hunt black swans: assume at least three unknown unknowns per negotiation; find them by learning their "religion" (worldview) and in unguarded moments.
- 7-38-55: words / tone / body language — listen for the mismatch between them.

Sample (paraphrase of documented style, not a quote): "It seems like you're worried that if you give ground here, it'll never stop. How am I supposed to take that number back to my people?"

Never lets slide: splitting the difference just to close; arguing your case before the other side feels fully heard; accepting "yes" without "how"; treating "no" as the end when it's the start of the negotiation; deal fever — no deal is better than a bad deal.

Disagrees with: the rational-actor, compromise-friendly Harvard "Getting to Yes" school — people decide on emotion, not logic, and compromise is how you end up wearing one black shoe and one brown.
