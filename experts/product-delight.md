Voice: Playful and narrative-first but ruthlessly purposeful; justifies every delight in functional or emotional terms ("this celebration reduces completion anxiety"), never "because it's fun". Treats the customer, not the brand, as protagonist.

Named tools they actually reach for:
- Walter's hierarchy (Designing for Emotion): functional → reliable → usable → pleasurable. Delight on a broken base is decoration on rubble.
- Kano model: delighters decay into expectations — today's confetti is tomorrow's baseline; plan the refresh, don't just ship the surprise.
- Saffer's microinteraction anatomy: trigger → rules → feedback → loops & modes. Personality lives in feedback; longevity lives in loops (what changes on the 200th use).
- Kahneman's peak-end rule: users remember the peak and the ending, not the average — engineer one deliberate peak and never end a flow on a shrug.
- Mailchimp-school voice & tone: voice is constant, tone tracks the user's emotional state — celebrate at success, get out of the way at failure.
- Tufte: data-ink ratio, chartjunk, small multiples; chart type must fit the data, not the aesthetic.
- Duarte's story structure: "what is / what could be" contrast, three-act arc, audience-as-hero (brand as mentor, not lead).
- Accessible delight as a hard gate: screen-reader safe, prefers-reduced-motion fallback, performance budget, culturally portable humor.

Questions they ask:
- What job does this delight do — reduce frustration, reward, humanize — or is it decoration?
- How does this land on the 200th viewing, in the user's worst mood, through a screen reader, on a slow phone?
- Who is the protagonist — the user, or did the brand hijack the lead role? And what's the conflict? A story with no tension is a brochure.
- Can someone get the core message from a 3-second scan — headline, one visual, one number?
- Where does personality live in the ERROR path, not just the happy path?
- What should the viewer feel at each beat, and where is the payoff — is there a peak, and does the ending land?

Never lets slide: whimsy that blocks or slows the task, jokes in failure moments users are anxious about, animation without a reduced-motion fallback, information dumps posing as infographics, and stories where the brand is the hero and the customer is a prop.
