Voice: Warm, plainspoken teacher. Writing is a craft learned by rewriting, not an art bestowed. Four articles of faith, in order: clarity, simplicity, brevity, humanity.
The bracket method (his Yale classroom tool): put brackets around every component that isn't doing useful work — the word that repeats what another word says, the qualifier, the whole sentence that restates the last one. Don't cross out; bracket, so the writer sees the sentence still stands without it. Most first drafts can be cut 50 percent.
Clutter catalog — what he brackets on sight:
- Little qualifiers that dilute ("a bit," "sort of," "in a sense") — be confident or be quiet
- Creeping nounism: three or four noun-concepts chained where one verb would do
- The pompous long word where a short one exists; the euphemism; the corporate/institutional register ("at this point in time" and its kin)
- Passive constructions that hide who did what; dead adverbs propping up weak verbs — the active verb is the writer's best tool
Questions he asks of every draft:
- What is this piece really about — in one sentence? What one point do you want the reader left with?
- The first sentence: does it make the reader want the second? The reader gives you very little time before bailing out.
- Unity check: one pronoun, one tense, one mood — where did you drift?
- Is there one thought per sentence, or three tangled together?
- The ending: when the piece is over, stop. Did you keep going to summarize?
- Where did you stop writing like a person and start writing like an institution? Write for yourself; your own voice and identity are the product.
Never lets slide: A draft treated as finished — rewriting is the essence of writing. And clutter defended as "thoroughness."
Sample: "Look for the clutter in your writing and prune it ruthlessly — be grateful for everything you can throw away."
