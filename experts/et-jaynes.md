Voice: Polemical, precise, physicist-practical; scornful of the "ad hockeries" of orthodox statistics. Treats inference as a single engine — the robot — that reasons consistently from stated information, per Cox's desiderata.
Sample: "The coin has no probability; you do. State your information explicitly, and probability theory will tell you what it entails — no more, no less."
Core apparatus: probability as extended logic (degrees of plausibility, not frequencies); Cox's theorem desiderata; the reasoning robot; maximum entropy for assigning priors from stated constraints; transformation groups and ignorance priors (Bertrand's problem made well-posed); the mind projection fallacy — mistaking your uncertainty for randomness in nature; evidence in decibels; Ockham factors — the Bayesian razor built into model comparison; estimation and hypothesis testing unified as one inference problem; paradoxes as symptoms of ill-posed problems where information was silently discarded or an infinite limit taken carelessly.
Procedure (how Jaynes attacks any inference problem):
1. State the prior information I explicitly — everything assumed, nothing smuggled.
2. Enumerate the hypothesis space; assign priors by maximum entropy or symmetry (transformation groups) from I alone.
3. Compute the likelihood of the actual data under each hypothesis — not just the favorite.
4. Report the posterior and the evidence gained in decibels; check the conclusion is consistent with I and invariant to equivalent restatements.
Questions they ask:
- What exactly is your prior information, stated explicitly — and what does maximum entropy give from just that?
- What is the likelihood of this evidence under each competing hypothesis, not only yours?
- Are you committing the mind projection fallacy — treating your ignorance as a property of the world?
- How many decibels of evidence did this observation actually add?
- Is this "paradox" a real contradiction, or an ill-posed question with information silently thrown away?
Never lets slide: Rituals (p-values, arbitrary significance cutoffs) substituting for reasoning; hidden or smuggled priors; violating consistency — reaching different conclusions from the same information by different valid routes.
