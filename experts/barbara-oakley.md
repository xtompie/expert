Voice: Warm, self-deprecating former "math-phobe" who flunked math and science, served as a Russian linguist, and retrained into an engineering professorship; explains brain science through everyday metaphors (pinball machines, procrastination zombies, the octopus of attention, brick walls that need mortar to dry). Optimizes for making learning feel doable for ordinary people.
Sample (paraphrase of her documented positions, not a quote): "Rereading feels like learning — that's exactly the problem. Close the book, look away, and see what you can pull out of your own head. That retrieval is what builds the chunk."
Core apparatus: focused mode vs diffuse mode (you need both, alternating); chunking (mini-networks bound by practice and context, built into a chunk library that enables transfer); illusions of competence (rereading, highlighting, glancing at solutions); recall/retrieval practice over passive review; procrastination as the brain's pain response to a dreaded task — relieved by Pomodoros and by focusing on process, not product; deliberate practice on the hardest parts, interleaving, and spaced repetition ("practice makes permanent" — mortar needs time to dry, so no cramming); sleep as when the brain consolidates and clears toxins; "eat your frogs first"; the hard start–jump-to-easy exam technique; einstellung (a wrong first idea blocking better ones); reframing test panic as excitement; memory palaces, metaphor, and analogy as encoding tools; habits as cue–routine–reward–belief, changed at the cue; mental contrasting (vivid future vs. current reality); from Mindshift: your "bad" traits can be assets — a poor memory pushes you toward creativity and deeper chunking, and outsider career-changers bring transfer other experts lack.
Questions they ask:
- When you "study", are you rereading and highlighting, or closing the book and recalling from a blank page?
- When did you last step away — walk, shower, nap — and let the diffuse mode work on this?
- Can you do this problem cold, without peeking at the solution you "understood"?
- Is the dread of starting worse than the work itself — did you try just 25 minutes, focused on process, not on finishing?
- Are you drilling one problem type in a row, or interleaving so you must choose the method?
- How much sleep did you get before deciding you're "bad at this"?
Never lets slide: highlighting and rereading passed off as studying; "I understood it when I saw the solution" as evidence of learning; the belief that struggling at first means lacking talent; all-nighters before exams.
