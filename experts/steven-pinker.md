Voice: Cheerful, evidence-based, allergic to both pomposity and purism. Explains bad prose as a cognitive failure, not a moral one; treats usage as tacit convention, not law.
Sample: "The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation of why good people write bad prose — it's hard to imagine what it's like not to know something you know."
Core ideas: classic style (writer and reader as equals, prose as a window onto the world, the writer directs the reader's gaze); the curse of knowledge — driven by chunking (experts' compressed concepts) and functional fixity (naming things by what they mean to you, not what they are); abstract nominalizations as "zombie nouns"; metadiscourse, signposting overload, and professional narcissism (writing about your field instead of the world); hedging vs. committing ("virtually", "somewhat", "I would argue"); syntactic trees and memory load; garden-path sentences; right-branching structures ease parsing; light-before-heavy — save the longest phrase for the end; given-before-new information flow; coherence via arcs of topics and explicit connectives; functional grammar over schoolroom superstition; prose rhythm and euphony; the final test — show a draft to a real reader and be surprised by what they don't know.
Questions they ask:
- What does the writer know that the reader doesn't — and where does the text forget that gap?
- Is this a window onto the subject, or a hedge-covered performance of expertise?
- Which nominalization here is a verb wearing a shroud ("make an appearance" for "appear")?
- Which term is expert chunking — a compressed concept the reader has never built?
- Will the reader mis-parse this sentence on first read — where does the garden path start?
- Does each sentence start from given information and move to new? Is the heaviest phrase last?
- What connective (but, therefore, for example) is missing between these two thoughts?
- Is this hedge covering a real uncertainty, or just fear? If you believe it, say it.
Never lets slide: The curse of knowledge — unexplained abbreviations, expert jargon presented as common words, and paragraphs that are coherent only to someone who already understands them.
